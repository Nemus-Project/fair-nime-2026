\section{Introduction}

Over the past decade there has been a growing movement in academia towards adopting an ``open methodology'' \cite{nosek_scientific_2012}, open science
 \cite{unesco_open_2021} and FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reproducible) \cite{go_fair_2016} digital research.
The adoption of these principles can be put under the umbrella of `open research practice,' which is vital to ensure transparency, validation, re-use and access within academic research.
The movement has led to an increased awareness across research communities and the establishment of accepted best-practice frameworks \cite{smith_software_2016}. The focus of these frameworks has predominantly been towards digital research practice in the sciences \cite{unesco_open_2021}.
but is beginning to see adoption within the humanities.


\textbf{Central Argument:} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item there is educational value in recreating and iterating on previous work \cite{dasgupta_remixing_2016}. 
    \item Creative applications of technology fosters learning of technical skills \cite{sharmin_creativity_2021, marin_computational_2024}. 
    \item There is a (growing) open source movement at NIME
    \item Authors are having to decide the format in how they disseminate source repositories. 
    \item but the work can sometimes be too tricky to find. 
    \item NIME is potentially missing an opportunity to engage with a community of new developers, 
    \item Inaction is easier than action, which might explain under adoption.
    \item the contents of a repository don't need to be perfect, sharing is ``good enough.''     
\end{itemize}

Ambiguity in how to disseminate source creates friction in the process of finding it and as a result it will be more difficult to engage with it.

Paper discusses how open research looks at current open research practice at NIME and what could be developed an the missed opportunities to engage with wider community of new creative developers.

\subsection{Current State of Open DMIs}\label{sec:current-state}

In the cours of instructing university students, the NIME archive has been a valuable resource for exploring creative applications on the use of micro-controllers, electronics and computer programming. NIME has 2000+ projects available in its open-access archive from which students can draw inspirations and iterate upon. Though many of the projects are open source, far too often this is only on condition that the source can be located in the first place. Prompted by the analysis in \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, two questions were raised. The first question is ``For all papers in NIME, for how many can the source be navigated to from the paper directly?'' Secondly, ``to what degree has open source practice been adopted at NIME?'' In particular, since the initial discussion of NIMEhub in 2016. Through answering these question, we may find a method in which we can foster greater open research practice and subsequently engage with a wider community outside of NIME.

\subsubsection{Question 1: Navigating to Source}

To answer the first question, a search was conducted on the NIME proceedings of 2025. 
Open source research practice has changed since 2001 and the guidance for best-practice academia has developed, from the recommendations in academic open science \cite{go_fair_2016,unesco_open_2021}, open hardware \cite{oshwa_best_2013} and more specifically open research practice at NIME \cite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016,jensenius_open_2019,fiordelmondo_longevity_2024}.
NIME 2025 should therefore represent the `bleeding edge' of open research practice as it will has been informed from guidance available up until this point.

The method used by \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and Review B from \citet{fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} were used as a starting point search for inclusion of a URL in a paper that would lead the reader to the project's source material. 
It was not critical that the source be buildable, executable or even complete, but rather that there had been a clear intention from the author(s) to make the project open by sharing some additional material in a repository representing the work of the paper.
The search aimed to be as forgiving as possible and any completely automated search would like miss instances where the source URLs were not immediately apparent \cite{nime2025_21,nime2025_32}.

The search started with wildcard ``git*'' which would match git URLs and usage of terms GitHub and GitLab that may be used to identify a repository link. If no results were found, additional searches were made using keywords ``source,'' ``code,'' ``http,''  and ``doi''. Upon failing to find any result, the paper was manually skim read to identify if an alternate method was use to reference the source repository. If a link to source material was found, the URL was recorded alongside the context in which it appeared as well as the page number. NIME 2025 consisted of a total of 96 papers. This was small enough that a semi-manual approach of the entire proceedings was achievable in just over three hours.

Cases where the hyperlink was broken in the PDF, but could be easily ascertained and written manually, qualified \cite{nime2025_58, nime2025_62}.

Of the 96 papers from NIME 2025, there are a total of 34 that have an intention of sharing their source, be it software, datasets or even zines \cite{nime2025_42}. This is in keeping with the 33\% reported in \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} which applied a similar process to papers from 2018--2020.
Overwhelmingly GitHub is the preferred repository platform with 30 projects hosting a repository there, which was the trend identified in \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}. Broken down further, 7 papers provided a URL in footnote on the first page and the remaining 11 in a footnote on a later page.

Analysis to answer the second question was based on the method in \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, which showed the occurrence of terms related to open source repositories, such as ``git'' and ``repository'' in NIME proceedings from 2001 to 2024.
Results from a method based on occurrence could be skewed where there is prolific use of repositories by an individual paper. The familiarity with git version control in NIME would more appropriately evaluated by examining the number of papers that reference a git repository.

% Since the search was based on occurrence, it could be skewed by individual with prolific use or citation of open source repositories. 
% What is important is perhaps not the number of occurrences, but whether a paper has relied on the technology at all. 

To achieve this, the PDFs for NIME proceedings from 2016 to 2025 were downloaded, separated into directories by year.
Each directory was search for the number of files in which there was a reference to a git repository. The search was carried out using the open source \texttt{pdfgrep} software \cite{deifel_pdfgrep_2024} and case-insensitive regular expression of \texttt{\textbackslash Wgit}. The search would match occurrences of `git' and variants like `\textbf{Git}Hub' and `\textbf{Git}Lab' as well as their urls  like \texttt{https:/\textbf{/git}lab.com/}. One deviation from \cite{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} was to include \texttt{github.io} webpages in the results as it would suggest usage or familiarity with GitHub and git software. It is not uncommon for authors to link to their own project via a GitHub hosted webpage (e.g., \texttt{user.github.io/project}) from which the repository URL can be inferred if not linked to directly on the site. The number of NIME papers referencing a git repository, and the percentage of the total they represent, is shown in Table~\ref{tab:git_occurences}.  This search approach is not foolproof and can miss instances, such as in \citet{carter_sculpting_2025}, where a project repository exists but neither the term `git' appears in the URL nor is it referenced directly in the text.

This section discusses the open methodology applied to the interface described in \citet{hamilton_augmentation_2025_activities}. The interface was created as part of the NEMUS project, which has a dedication to making all digital research products open source.
From early on it was apparent that given the involvement of the project, simple open sourcing was not enough and the project aimed to incorporate best open research practices in the early stages of development \cite{chen_open_2019}.

Using the \citep{ssi_checklist_2018} checklist, the core requirements for an open research
software project are: publicly available source code, a version control system
(VCS), user and developer documentation, an OSI-compliant licence, and
persistent identifiers (typically DOIs). A public VCS implies source
availability, though the reverse is not guaranteed. Likewise, a DOI implies some
level of digital archiving, but a release archive does not necessitate a DOI.
DOIs should be assigned both to the software as a whole and to individual
releases \cite{smith_software_2016}; licences should be clearly stated and
OSI-compliant \cite{hong_choosing_2025}. To better support complex software
citation, \citet{druskat_citation_2021} introduced the Citation File Format
(CFF), which standardises metadata for software citations and is supported by
both GitHub and Zenodo. Recommendations in \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}
extend this to hardware projects, with tools for citation
\cite{spaaks_cffconvert_2021}. Beyond these essentials (contribution policies,
issue tracking, codes of conduct), more advanced practices presuppose an active
repository.

Five key areas were identified that constitute `best-practice' in open research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Source: Defining all the components that constitute the project source
    \item Version Control: the system for  tracking changes to the source over time
    \item Archive: the system for digitally archiving for longevity and citablility through digital object identifier (DOI).
    \item Documentation: Tutorials, Reference, Explanation, How-To guides
    \item License: Terms under which the work can be used, modified, and shared 
\end{enumerate}

Much of the open sourcing had to carried out as the project developed. This resulted in some instances where what was initially implemented was no longer relevant or must be adapted. 
Rather than a single, unified code base \cite{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} the source of project was divided into three components --- \emph{Firmware} \cite{hamilton_firmware_2025}, \emph{CAD} \cite{hamilton_cad_2025}, and \emph{Model Data} \cite{hamilton_models_2025}. Such an approach aligns closely with the FAIR principles as it promotes code reuse, interoperability, as well as seamless attribution.
For the original paper the links to the individual project repositories were included. As the project developed, two distinct interfaces were created with different applications. One interface was for installation at San Colombano while a second model was created for use within research at the NEMUS project and the University of Bologna. Both interfaces use the same core components, but at separate phases in their development.

This means that the original links are now no longer an accurate representation of the interface as presented in the paper. To address this, the individual repositories were reference under a single `meta repository' (Figure~\ref{fig:repo-relation}). Referencing is achieved using git's submodules functionality \cite{git_submodule_2025}. The benefit of such an approach is two fold. 
To navigate the different states of the project in time, only a single URL needs to be shared. The individual submodules can be updated or swapped out entirely.
Version tagging is used to demarcate distinct versions of the interface, allowing for easier navigation.
Shallow submodules allow for only the relevant code for that commit to be pulled from the upstream repository meaning the full data weight of the entire repository is not needed.

The function of each component also shifted over time. The CAD component transitioned into files for electronic schematics and PCB manufacture, while the Model component has changed into a repository of documents describing measurements of the enclosure. Renaming either repository would break previous links to the work. The directory name of submodules can be changed in the meta repository, but this situation emphasised the importance of archiving the work.
Rather than referencing through URLs to a git repository, the project was archived using CERN's digital archiving platform Zenodo \cite{zenodo_2013}. Archiving exposed a few practical challenges. Zenodo’s GitHub integration does not yet handle meta-repositories, requiring separate DOIs for each submodule with cross-references \cite{zenodo_1036_2025} (Figure~\ref{fig:zenodo-github-integration}).
Likewise, the future of CAD data is tied to software sustainability: the discontinuation of Autodesk EAGLE \cite{autodesk_eagle_2024} effectively freezes its XML format, though conversion to KiCad remains available \cite{kicad_eagle_2024}. These lessons emphasise that openness depends not only on version control and archiving but also on the careful selection of formats and hosting infrastructure.

\subsection{Future Discussion}

Impactful on future engagement is archiving practice.

% What is a clear order of precedence for citing sowftare?
A clear order of precedence

\begin{itemize}
\item DOI to the archive of the relevant version
\item SoftWare Hash IDentifier (SWHID) \cite{softwareheritage_archive_2025}
\item Citation File Format \cite{druskat_citation_2021}
\item Suggested citation format
\item Version-specific repository URL
\item General project URL
\end{itemize}

When citing software or data, precedence should favour a citation that prioritises longevity and version specificity over those that merely indicate location.

SoftWare Hash IDentifier (SWHID) ISO/IEC international standard 18670 on April 23, 2025.
Is passive, requires the repository be public and cannot guarantee that submodules have also been ingested. 

Like the web archive, this should be looked upon as a last ditch and a much more active and deliberate archiving approach should be taken

A DMI as a research product \cite{xxx} is an object that should be citable. The paper describes the object, it is not object itself and there are instances where citing the object is more applicable than citing the paper based around it.

Archiving should provide a DOI with which to cite. Repositories may also provide a recommended citation, but this should very much be looked upon as a bandage to a broken system and not a solution. The very helpful citation file format CFF provides a means to have a structure citation to software, but unhelpfully, integration with GitHub prompts the user to ``Cite this Repository'' \url{https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-citation-files} which, as already discussed, is something to be avoided.
