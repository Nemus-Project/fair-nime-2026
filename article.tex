Over the past decade, academia has increasingly embraced ``open methodology'' \cite{nosek_scientific_2012}, encompassing open science \cite{unesco_open_2021} and FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reproducible) digital research principles \cite{go_fair_2016}. 
Collectively described here as as `open research practice,' these approaches are essential for ensuring transparency, validation, reuse, and access within scholarly work. 
This movement has raised awareness across research communities and led to the development of widely accepted best-practice frameworks \cite{smith_software_2016}. 
While these frameworks have primarily focused on scientific research \cite{unesco_open_2021}, their adoption is increasingly evident within the humanities \cite{jensenius_best_2021}.

% The FAIR principles and open science extend beyond supporting the longevity and reuse of digital research; they also enable broader engagement, study, and recreation of research at the educational level. Despite these benefits, open research practice is not yet standard within the humanities \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. One means of addressing this gap is through the teaching of creative technological applications, which have been shown to be an effective approach for developing technical skills \cite{sharmin_creativity_2021,marin_computational_2024}.

The FAIR principles and open science are not just a benefit to the longevity and reuse of digital academic research, but they also allow for wider engagement, study and recreation  of research at an educational level. 
Despite these benefits, open research practice is not yet standard practice within the humanities \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. 
The underlying materials of research—such as datasets, source code, design files, and other artefacts that are often regarded as disposable—are, in fact, critical for understanding how a project was constructed and the rationale behind key decisions. These research outputs embed tacit knowledge of engineering practice \cite{schindler_expertise_2015}, knowledge that is seldom explicitly documented yet remains highly valuable for skill development and future comprehension.


As a student, there is educational value and knowledge to be gained in recreating and iterating on work as a means of learning skills through practice \cite{dasgupta_remixing_2016}. 
The benefits of `apprenticing' through DMI design and fabrication have already been demonstrated \cite{nime2021_26}.
Currently, there is no common framework for organising or sharing a paper's source material, making it harder to find and engage with the work. This paper examines current open research practices within NIME, identifying areas for further development and highlighting missed opportunities to engage a broader community of emerging creative developers.


This paper first reviews work within the NIME community that addresses open research practice. 
That work is then used to establish a framework for analysing the use of open source at NIME through 2025. 
This is followed by a case study from NIME 2025 that examines the open source practices employed during and after the conference. Based on this analysis and case study, the paper concludes with recommendations aimed at strengthening open research practice and enabling NIME work to be more readily engaged with by a wider community of students, creators and researchers.

\section{Related Work}
% \section{Open Research at NIME}

Since its inception, NIME has been committed to making its proceedings openly accessible under a CC BY 4.0 licence.
In parallel, research projects within the NIME community have developed a strong tradition of open research practice, accompanied by ongoing discussions on how to better support, formalise, and sustain open source culture \cite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016}.
Questions of replication \cite{calegario_documentation_2021}, iteration \cite{nime2021_4}, and long-term sustainability \cite{fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} have been recurring themes at NIME.
This section reviews both past discussions and current practice in order to identify opportunities for strengthening open research practices within the NIME community.

\citet{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} presented one of the earliest discussions of open and reproducible research practices within the NIME community. Among the workshop’s eight primary goals were several aims adjacent to the FAIR principles, including encouraging reproducibility, promoting open access, and normalising documentation practices. A central focus of the workshop was the proposal of a shared repository for DMI designs. Although the NIMEhub itself did not ultimately materialise, the workshop catalysed ongoing discussion around open research at NIME \cite{jensenius_open_2019, jensenius_nime_2020}.

Subsequent discussions of open research at NIME have largely focused on methodologies relevant to open hardware \citep{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, particularly the accessibility of electronic designs that enable fabrication beyond a central manufacturer. Unlike software, open hardware cannot be effectively distributed solely through licensing and source files \citep{oshwa_best_2013} without risking ``openwashing,'' whereby a project is claimed to be open source despite the absence of published product-related information \citep{bonvoisin_source_2017}. In practice, supporting hardware reproducibility requires access to well-archived source materials, which significantly lowers the barriers to understanding, reconstructing, and iterating upon the original design.

Both \citet{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} highlight the importance of repository structure, with the latter proposing a prescriptive framework applicable across DMI projects. Research outputs in the form of open hardware or open software can, and should, leverage existing platforms designed for the distribution and maintenance of such work.

Discussions of ``recreating'' a NIME have predominantly emphasised its role in musical practice \citep{ajin_rebuilding_2019, masu_nime_2023, west_towards_2025}. However, an equally valid motivation for recreation lies in acquiring operational knowledge and developing craft through the act of building itself \citep{calegario_documentation_2021}, rather than solely through performance or use \citep{magnusson_epistemic_2009}.


\section{Analysis of Open Source Adoption at NIME}\label{sec:current-state}

% The NIME archive currently provides a valuable resource for those wishing to explore creative applications of micro-controllers, electronics and computer programming. 
% NIME has 2000+ projects available in its open-access archive from which students can draw inspiration and iterate upon. 
The NIME archive hosts 2,000+ open-access projects that support exploration and iteration in creative microcontroller, electronics, and programming practices.
Though many of the projects are open source, far too often this is only on condition that the source can be located in the first place.
Prompted by the analysis in \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, two questions were raised. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item ``How often do NIME papers link to source code?'' 
    \item ``How prevalent is open-source practice at NIME?'' 
\end{enumerate}
% Firtly, ``For all papers in NIME, for how many can the source be navigated to from the paper directly?'' 
% Secondly, ``to what degree has open source practice been adopted at NIME?'' 
Answering these questions may reveal a method that encourages open research practice and fosters engagement with a wider community beyond NIME.

Both questions would be answered through a search of the NIME proceedings up to 2025. 
This first required the download of all PDF files in the NIME paper archive, which were organised into subdirectories by year.
The text of the PDF files would then be searched using the the open source software \texttt{pdfgrep} \cite{deifel_pdfgrep_2024}.

\subsection{Question 1: Navigating to Source}

To answer the first question, a search was conducted on the NIME proceedings from 2020 to 2025.
Open source research practice has evolved since 2001, and guidance for best practices in academia has matured through recommendations in open science \cite{go_fair_2016,unesco_open_2021}, open hardware \cite{oshwa_best_2013}, and, more specifically, open research practice at NIME \cite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016,jensenius_open_2019,fiordelmondo_longevity_2024}.  
Therefore, NIME 2020–2025 should showcase the strongest examples of open research practice.

The presence of a URL in a paper --- that links to source material --- was taken as a marker of the authors' intention to make the project accessible. Using this criterion, we followed the approach of \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and Review B from \citet{fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} as the framework for a source material URL search. It was not necessary that the source be buildable, executable, or complete—only that there was a clear effort to share additional material in a repository representing the work described in the paper.

Over the past decade, the git version control system (VCS) has become a de facto standard for source repositories, making source material links easier to identify.
Nevertheless, the search was designed to be as forgiving as was practical, since not all papers contain or reference the term ``git'' or its variants with respect to their source repositories (e.g., \cite{carter_sculpting_2025, nime2025_32}).

The search began with the regular expression ``\textbackslash Wgit,'' which matches references to, and URLs of, repositories using the Git version control system (VCS).
If no results were found, additional searches were made using keywords ``source,'' ``code,'' ``http,''  and ``doi''. 
Each year's proceedings, from 2020 to 2025, were searched individually.
When a link to a source repository was found, the URL and the context in which it was used were recorded, and the paper was removed from the search pool.
Upon failing to find any result, the remaining papers from that year were manually read to identify if an alternate method was used to reference the source repository. 
NIME 2020 -- 2025 consisted of a total of 532 papers. This was a small enough number that a semi-manual approach of the entire proceedings was achievable in just over ten hours. 
Since the process was partially manual, the figures reported here are representative rather than conclusive. 
There is a possibility of papers that referenced their repositories using alternative methods being overlooked, though an effort has been made to identify them.

To answer the second question, all NIME paper proceedings were searched for references to a git repository with a case-insensitive regular expression \texttt{\textbackslash Wgit}, matching occurrences of git and variants such as `GitHub' and `GitLab', as well as URLs like \texttt{https:/\textbf{/git}hub.com/}. 
This approach follows the method of \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, which counted papers containing terms related to open source repositories from NIME proceedings 2001--2024. 
One deviation from the previous method was inclusion \texttt{github.io} links. The \texttt{github.io} URL was also  included since authors sometimes link to project pages hosted there and familiarity with git and GitHub can be inferred.  
The resulting yearly percentages of NIME papers referencing a Git repository are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:git_occurences}.
% Spike in 2023 may be partially answered with that years theme of `Frugal Music Innovation' emphasising `solving problems with the available resources' a theme with which usage of open source projects would fit.
There is substantial overlap between git as a technology and GitHub as a platform, suggesting that most interactions with the git VCS occur via GitHub.
Put in context of Table~\ref{tab:git_placement}, the gap between usage of open source projects and publishing a projects source has gradually increased since NIME 2020.


This search approach is not foolproof and can miss instances where a project repository exists but neither the term `git' appears in the URL nor is it referenced directly in the text.
The search was run multiple times over a directory containing all NIME papers to date, with progressively stricter constraints applied to reduce false positives. The process began with a \texttt{pdfgrep} search, which removed the majority of genuine matches and enabled more efficient pruning of the remaining false positives.%
\footnote{\texttt{pdfgrep -P '\textbackslash Wgit(?!hub|lab)' -ri .}}

Examples of false positives were words split across line breaks at “git” (e.g., legitimation broken into ``le-'' and ``\textbf{git}imation''), as well as unrelated terms such as ``gitaroo'' or ``gitarre.''
The search could not be overly restrictive, however, because it needed to retain valid matches such as ``gitorious,'' a git-based hosting service that was acquired by GitLab and discontinued in 2015 \cite{bjaanes_gitorious_2015}.



\section{An Open Methodology for DMIs}

Section~\ref{sec:current-state} shows that open source \emph{use} within NIME has grown steadily, yet the ability to \emph{locate} and \emph{reuse} project materials remains inconsistent. Even when authors intend to share, repository links may be missing, fragile, or indirect (Table~\ref{tab:git_placement}), and a non-trivial portion of projects can only be recovered through manual inference or external search. This adoption--practice gap suggests that the primary barrier is not a lack of willingness to be open, but rather the absence of shared conventions for packaging, signposting, and preserving the heterogeneous materials that constitute a DMI---firmware, schematics, enclosure models, datasets, and documentation. In other words, the challenge is less about whether a project is ``open'' in principle, and more about whether its constituent research products are \emph{findable and reconstructible} in practice. This becomes especially salient for DMIs, where reproducibility depends on multiple interdependent artefacts and where tacit knowledge is encoded across tools, file formats, and incremental design decisions. The remainder of the paper, therefore, shifts from measuring the current state of open source referencing in the proceedings to demonstrating a concrete, end-to-end methodology for making a DMI's source navigable and citable over time.


The case study is the open methodology applied to the interface described in \anon{\citet{hamilton_augmentation_2025_activities}}. The interface was created as part of \anon{the NEMUS project}, which is dedicated to making most digital research products open source. From early on, it was apparent that, given the project's involvement, simple open sourcing was not enough, and the project aimed to incorporate best open research practices from the outset \cite{chen_open_2019}.

According to the \citet{ssi_checklist_2018} checklist, the core requirements for an open research
software project are: publicly available source code, a VCS, user and developer documentation, an Open Source Institute (OSI) compliant licence \cite{osi_licenses_2025}, and
persistent digital object identifiers (DOI). A public VCS implies source
availability, though the reverse is not guaranteed. Likewise, a DOI implies some
level of digital archiving, but a release archive does not necessitate a DOI.
DOIs should be assigned both to the software as a whole and to individual
releases \cite{smith_software_2016}; licences should be clearly stated and
OSI-compliant \cite{hong_choosing_2025}. To better support complex software
citation, \citet{druskat_citation_2021} introduced the Citation File Format
(CFF), which standardises metadata for software citations and is supported by
both GitHub and Zenodo. Recommendations in \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}
extend this to hardware projects, with tools for citation
\cite{spaaks_cffconvert_2021}. Beyond these essentials (contribution policies,
issue tracking, codes of conduct), more advanced practices presuppose an active
repository.

Five key areas were identified that constitute `best-practice' in open research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Source: Defining all the components that constitute the project source.
    \item Version Control: the system for  tracking changes to the source over time.
    \item Archive: the system for digitally archiving for longevity and citability through digital object identifier (DOI).
    \item Documentation: Tutorials, Reference, Explanation, How-To guides.
    \item License: Terms under which the work can be used, modified, and shared.
\end{enumerate}

Much of the open sourcing had to be carried out as the project developed. This resulted in some instances in which what was initially implemented was no longer relevant or needed to be adapted.
Rather than a single, unified code base \cite{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_longevity_2024}, the source of project was divided into three components 
\anon{--- \emph{Firmware} \cite{hamilton_firmware_2025}, \emph{CAD} \cite{hamilton_cad_2025}, and \emph{Model Data} \cite{hamilton_models_2025}}. 
Such an approach aligns closely with the FAIR principles as it promotes code reuse, interoperability, as well as seamless attribution.
For the original paper, the links to the individual project repositories were included. As the project developed, two distinct interfaces were created with different applications. One interface was for \anon{installation at San Colombano}, while a second model was created for use in research within the \anon{NEMUS project and the University of Bologna}. Both interfaces use the same core components, but at separate phases in their development.

This means the original links no longer accurately reflect the interface as presented in the paper. To address this, the individual repositories were referenced under a single `meta repository' (Figure~\ref{fig:repo-relation}). Referencing is achieved using git's submodules functionality \cite{git_submodule_2025}. The benefit of such an approach is two-fold.
To navigate the project's states over time, only a single URL needs to be shared. The individual submodules can be updated or swapped out entirely.
Version tagging demarcates distinct interface versions, making navigation easier.

The function of each component also shifted over time. The CAD component transitioned into files for electronic schematics and PCB manufacture, while the Model component has changed into a repository of documents describing measurements of the enclosure. Renaming either repository would break previous links to the work. The directory name of submodules can be changed in the meta repository, but this situation emphasised the importance of archiving the work.
Rather than referencing through URLs to a git repository, the project was archived using CERN's digital archiving platform Zenodo \cite{zenodo_2013}. Archiving exposed a few practical challenges. Zenodo’s GitHub integration does not yet handle meta-repositories, requiring separate DOIs for each submodule with cross-references \cite{zenodo_1036_2025} (Figure~\ref{fig:zenodo-github-integration}).
Likewise, the future of CAD data is tied to software sustainability: the discontinuation of Autodesk EAGLE \cite{autodesk_eagle_2024} effectively freezes its XML format, though conversion to KiCad remains available \cite{kicad_eagle_2024}. These lessons emphasise that openness depends not only on version control and archiving but also on the careful selection of formats and hosting infrastructure.

Moving forward one potential change that can be made to the NIME proceedings and paper submission process is the inclusion of a `project URL' field as a standard feature of the template and metadata (Figure~\ref{fig:project-details}). The benefits of such a change would be manifold.
To measure the impact of a NIME in a wider community something needs to be tracked.
A project's source repository and its URL provide a means by which it usage can be tracked, but identifying them in the first place is time consuming especially if it is to be rigorous.
Both \citet{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_towards_2025} undertake very involved searches of the NIME archive to figure out if there is even a repository. It might be time to simplify the process of analysing open source practice at NIME. In the same manner that the NIME proceedings analyser can \cite{goode_toolkit_2022,nime2024_3} trivially extract authors and title data, the same should also be true for the location of the paper's source.

Table~\ref{tab:git_placement} illustrates that the manner in which source is shared is not standardised. Providing a standard field removes some burden from the author to interpret what would be the appropriate way to point to the work. In fact, a standard field would strengthen authorial intent as it removes ambiguity if a repository URL is the project source or simply related to the project in some manner.
This should hopefully reduce the growing number of instances where a project URL is invalid, either through the repository being behind authentication, set to `private,' empty or missing entirely, acting as it would a memory aid.

Asking authors for a project URL may also provide a reminder to share there work when a repository is extant, but the opportunity to reference it directly has not arisen or has been cut, as is all too easy during the paper writing and editing process. This can be enforces in the same manner as the \texttt{Description} field for figures of the current NIME paper template \cite{nime_template_2023}.

Whether this would make a difference, only implementation and time will tell.
The standard could be applied retroactively to the existing NIME archive.
The data collected as part of this paper and its state at time of submission are openly available \anon{\cite{hamilton_fair_2026, hamilton_fair_archive_2026}} to assist that process.

Finally, the burden is removed from the reader to identify where the source code for project is located. 
If the NIME community needs to put in a large amount of effort to find it, then the same will be true for the readership and a wider audience.
NIMEs provide a resource and archive for instructing students in creative engineering, electronics,  musicianship and HCI.
By making it harder for students to engage \cite{nime2021_26}, remix \cite{dasgupta_remixing_2016} and foster creativity \cite{sharmin_creativity_2021, marin_computational_2024}, NIME may be missing out on a new community of contributors.

Discussion of open source practice also raises broader issues of alignment with FAIR principles and digital project archiving. Given the distributed nature of git, there is more resilience to the problem of data loss. GitHub, as a standard, also has the infrastructure, but it should not be looked upon as a permanent storage solution, not for 20 or 30 years. The project source for a NIME needs to be citable \cite{smith_software_2016}, and cross-reference-able \cite{stall_journal_2023} if it is to be built upon. This is not just a salient topic for NIME, but all academic research. 

Digital archiving infrastructure exists in the form of \url{https://arxiv.org/} and \url{https://zenodo.org/} which are openly available. Zenodo is already utilised as the standard archive platform for NIME proceedings. 
For NIME 2025, 16 papers include video-only supplementary files, and one provides a citable digital archive with a DOI \cite{studley_swimtunes_2025}.
Those ``going beyond the PDF format'' \cite{calegario_documentation_2021} are still in the minority, and digital archiving remains an under-adopted standard practice. NIME has the potential to reach wider communities now, but it should also look towards the communities of the future.
