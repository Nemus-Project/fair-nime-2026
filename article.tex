Over the past decade, academia has increasingly embraced ``open methodology'' \cite{nosek_scientific_2012}, encompassing open science \cite{unesco_open_2021} and FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reproducible) digital research principles \cite{go_fair_2016}. 
Collectively described here as as `open research practice,' these approaches are essential for ensuring transparency, validation, reuse, and access within scholarly work. 
This movement has raised awareness across research communities and led to the development of widely accepted best-practice frameworks \cite{smith_software_2016}. 
While these frameworks have primarily focused on scientific research \cite{unesco_open_2021}, their adoption is increasingly evident within the humanities \cite{jensenius_best_2021}.

% The FAIR principles and open science extend beyond supporting the longevity and reuse of digital research; they also enable broader engagement, study, and recreation of research at the educational level. Despite these benefits, open research practice is not yet standard within the humanities \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. One means of addressing this gap is through the teaching of creative technological applications, which have been shown to be an effective approach for developing technical skills \cite{sharmin_creativity_2021,marin_computational_2024}.

The FAIR principles and open science are not just a benefit to the longevity and reuse of digital academic research, but they also allow for wider engagement, study and recreation  of research at an educational level. 
Despite these benefits, open research practice is not yet standard practice within the humanities \cite{taylor_shaping_2022}. 
The source materials — the datasets, source code, design files and other artifacts often treated as disposable by researchers — are actually essential for understanding how a project was built and why key decisions were made. These research products will have encoded within the tacit knowledge of engineering practice \citet{schindler_expertise_2015} that are rarely documented but valuable for skill acquisition and future understanding.

As a student, there is educational value and knowledge to be gained in recreating and iterating on work as a means of learning skills through practice \cite{dasgupta_remixing_2016}. 
The benefits of `apprenticing' through DMI design and fabrication have already been demonstrated \cite{nime2021_26}.
Currently, there is no common framework for organising or sharing a paper's source material, making it harder to find and engage with the work. This paper discusses how open research looks at current open research practice at NIME and what could be developed and the missed opportunities to engage with wider community of new creative developers.

\section{Open Research at NIME}

Since its inception, NIME has been committed to making its proceedings openly accessible under a CC BY 4.0 licence.
In parallel, research projects within the NIME community have developed a strong tradition of open research practice, accompanied by ongoing discussions on how to better support, formalise, and sustain open-source culture \cite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016}.
Questions of replication \cite{calegario_documentation_2021}, iteration \cite{nime2021_4}, and long-term sustainability \citet{fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} have been recurring themes at NIME.
This section reviews both past discussions and current practice in order to identify opportunities for strengthening open research practices within the NIME community.

\subsection{Previous Analysis}

\citet{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} from 2016 was one of the first discussions of an open and reproducible research practice. Part of that workshop's primary eight goals were the FAIR adjacent aims of encouraging reproducibility, open access and encouraging a normalised practice of documentation. 
A central topic for discussion during the workshop was the creation of a central repository for DMI designs. The NIMEhub did not ultimately manifest, but the workshop has sparked further discussion of open research at NIME \cite{jensenius_open_2019, jensenius_nime_2020}.
The discussion of open research at NIME has tended towards methodologies applicable to open hardware \citet{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, the provision of making electronics designs accessible allowing fabrication outside of a central manufacturer.
Unlike software, open hardware cannot be simply distributed as a licence and set of source code files \cite{oshwa_best_2013} lest it fall afoul of `openwashing,' when a project  ``is claimed to be open source whereas no intention to publish product-related information is given'' \cite{bonvoisin_source_2017}. In practice, enabling the reproduction of hardware requires access to archived source materials which substantially lowers the barrier to understanding, remaking, and iterating on the authors' original idea.

Both \citet{mcpherson_nimehub_2016} and \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025} raise the issue of repository structure with the latter proposing a prescriptive measure which can be applied to any DMI project. 
Research output in the form of open hardware or open software can and should take advantage of the platforms available for distribution of the work.
Discussions of "recreating" a NIME have consistently centred on its use in musical practice \cite{ajin_rebuilding_2019, masu_nime_2023, west_towards_2025}. 
Equally valid is recreating a NIME to gain operational knowledge and develop craft from through building \cite{calegario_documentation_2021} and not just through playing \cite{magnusson_epistemic_2009}.

\section{Analysis of Open Source Adoption at NIME}\label{sec:current-state}

In the course of instructing university students, the NIME archive has been a valuable resource for exploring creative applications on the use of micro-controllers, electronics and computer programming. NIME has 2000+ projects available in its open-access archive from which students can draw inspirations and iterate upon. Though many of the projects are open source, far too often this is only on condition that the source can be located in the first place. Prompted by the analysis in \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, two questions were raised. The first question is ``For all papers in NIME, for how many can the source be navigated to from the paper directly?''. Secondly, ``to what degree has open source practice been adopted at NIME?'' In particular, since the initial discussion of NIMEhub in 2016. Through answering these question, we may find a method in which we can foster greater open research practice and subsequently engage with a wider community outside of NIME.

\subsection{Question 1: Navigating to Source}

To answer the first question, a search was conducted on the NIME proceedings of 2025.
Open source research practice has changed since 2001 and the guidance for best-practice in academia has developed, from the recommendations in academic open science \cite{go_fair_2016,unesco_open_2021}, open hardware \cite{oshwa_best_2013} and more specifically open research practice at NIME \cite{mcpherson_nimehub_2016,jensenius_open_2019, fiordelmondo_longevity_2024}.

The presence of a URL in a paper was taken as a marker of the authors' intention to make the project accessible. Using this criterion, we followed the approach of \citet{calegario_documentation_2021} and Review B from \citet{fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} to guide our search for URLs pointing to project source material. It was not necessary that the source be buildable, executable, or complete—only that there was a clear effort to share additional material in a repository representing the work described in the paper.
Over the past decade, the git version control system (VCS) has become a de facto standard for source repositories,, making source material links easier to identify.
Nevertheless, the search was designed to be as forgiving as was practical, since not all papers contain or reference the term ``git'' or its variants with respect to their source repositories (e.g., \cite{carter_sculpting_2025, nime2025_32}).

The search started with the wildcard search ``git*'' which would match URLs of repositories using the git version control system (VCS). The wildcard matches usage of associated URLs for online git services, such as GitHub and GitLab, that may be used to identify a repository link. If no results were found, additional searches were made using keywords ``source,'' ``code,'' ``http,''  and ``doi''. Upon failing to find any result, the paper was manually skim read to identify if an alternate method was used to reference the source repository. If a link to source material was found, the URL was recorded alongside the context in which it appeared as well as the page number. NIME 2020 -- 2025 consisted of a total of 532 papers. This was small enough that a semi-manual approach of the entire proceedings was achievable in just over ten hours. Since the process was partially manual, the figures reported here are representative rather than conclusive. There is a possibility of referenced repositories using alternative methods being overlooked, though an effort has been made to count them.

The standards for what qualified as a link to the project source were deliberately broad.
Papers that included a URL to a web page that contained a subsequent link to source qualified \cite{nime2025_10,nime2025_16,carter_sculpting_2025,nime2025_37,nime2025_67}.
Cases where the hyperlink was broken in the PDF, but could be easily ascertained and written manually, qualified \cite{nime2025_58, nime2025_62}.

There were four instances where the repository was unreachable from the link, or the repository was completely empty. In one instance, the repository link was missing entirely, but a web search of the project name yielded the result in the first page. The authors were contacted, and four out of five have provided an alternative at the time of writing. These repositories all qualified and are included in the total.

Of the 96 papers from NIME 2025, there are a total of 34 that have an intention of sharing their source, be it software, datasets or even zines \cite{nime2025_42}. This is in keeping with the 33\% reported in \citet{calegario_documentation_2021}, which applied a similar process to papers from 2018--2020.
GitHub is by far the preferred repository platform (Table~\ref{tab:git_type}), hosting 32 projects and reflecting the trend observed by \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}. Table~\ref{tab:git_placement} shows how NIME 2025 authors disseminated source URLs, with footnotes being the most common method: 7 instances on the first page and 11 instances on later pages.

To answer the second question, we downloaded the PDFs of NIME proceedings from 2016 to 2025 and organised them into directories by year. Each directory was searched for references to a Git repository using the open-source software \texttt{pdfgrep} \cite{deifel_pdfgrep_2024} with a case-insensitive regular expression \texttt{\textbackslash Wgit}, matching occurrences of git' and variants such as `GitHub' and `GitLab', as well as URLs like \texttt{https:/\textbf{/git}hub.com/}. This approach follows the method of \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}, which counted papers containing terms related to open source repositories from NIME proceedings 2001--2024. One deviation from the previous method was inclusion \texttt{github.io} links. The \texttt{github.io} URL was also  included since authors sometimes link to project pages hosted there and familiarity with git and GitHub can be inferred.  
The resulting yearly percentages of NIME papers referencing a Git repository are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:git_occurences}.
% Spike in 2023 may be partially answered with that years theme of `Frugal Music Innovation' emphasising `solving problems with the available resources' a theme with which usage of open source projects would fit.
There is substantial overlap between git as a technology and GitHub as a platform, suggesting that most interactions with the git VCS occur via GitHub.
Put in context of Table~\ref{tab:git_placement}, the gap between usage of open source projects and publishing a projects source has gradually increased since NIME 2020.


This search approach is not foolproof and can miss instances, such as in \citet{carter_sculpting_2025}, where a project repository exists but neither the term `git' appears in the URL nor is it referenced directly in the text.
The search was run multiple times over a directory containing all NIME papers to date, with progressively stricter constraints applied to reduce false positives. The process began with a \texttt{pdfgrep} search, which removed the majority of genuine matches and enabled more efficient pruning of the remaining false positives.%
\footnote{\texttt{pdfgrep -P '\textbackslash Wgit(?!hub|lab)' -ri .}}

Examples of false positives were words split across line breaks at “git” (e.g., legitimation broken into ``le-'' and ``\textbf{git}imation''), as well as unrelated terms such as ``gitaroo'' or ``gitarre.''
The search could not be overly restrictive, however, because it needed to retain valid matches such as ``gitorious,'' a git-based hosting service that was acquired by GitLab and discontinued in 2015 \cite{bjaanes_gitorious_2015}.

This section discusses the open methodology applied to the interface described in \anon{\citet{hamilton_augmentation_2025_activities}}. The interface was created as part of the \anon{NEMUS} project, which has a dedication to making all digital research products open source.
From early on, it was apparent that, given the involvement of the project, simple open sourcing was not enough, and the project aimed to incorporate best open research practices in the early stages of development \cite{chen_open_2019}.

Using the \citep{ssi_checklist_2018} checklist, the core requirements for an open research
software project are: publicly available source code, a VCS, user and developer documentation, an Open Source Institute (OSI) compliant licence \cite{osi_licenses_2025}, and
persistent digital object identifiers (DOI). A public VCS implies source
availability, though the reverse is not guaranteed. Likewise, a DOI implies some
level of digital archiving, but a release archive does not necessitate a DOI.
DOIs should be assigned both to the software as a whole and to individual
releases \cite{smith_software_2016}; licences should be clearly stated and
OSI-compliant \cite{hong_choosing_2025}. To better support complex software
citation, \citet{druskat_citation_2021} introduced the Citation File Format
(CFF), which standardises metadata for software citations and is supported by
both GitHub and Zenodo. Recommendations in \citet{fiordelmondo_towards_2025}
extend this to hardware projects, with tools for citation
\cite{spaaks_cffconvert_2021}. Beyond these essentials (contribution policies,
issue tracking, codes of conduct), more advanced practices presuppose an active
repository.

Five key areas were identified that constitute `best-practice' in open research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Source: Defining all the components that constitute the project source.
    \item Version Control: the system for  tracking changes to the source over time.
    \item Archive: the system for digitally archiving for longevity and citability through digital object identifier (DOI).
    \item Documentation: Tutorials, Reference, Explanation, How-To guides.
    \item License: Terms under which the work can be used, modified, and shared.
\end{enumerate}

Much of the open sourcing had to be carried out as the project developed. This resulted in some instances where what was initially implemented was no longer relevant or must be adapted.
Rather than a single, unified code base \cite{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_longevity_2024} the source of project was divided into three components 
\anon{--- \emph{Firmware} \cite{hamilton_firmware_2025}, \emph{CAD} \cite{hamilton_cad_2025}, and \emph{Model Data} \cite{hamilton_models_2025}}. 
Such an approach aligns closely with the FAIR principles as it promotes code reuse, interoperability, as well as seamless attribution.
For the original paper, the links to the individual project repositories were included. As the project developed, two distinct interfaces were created with different applications. One interface was for \anon{installation at San Colombano} while a second model was created for use within research at the \anon{NEMUS project and the University of Bologna}. Both interfaces use the same core components, but at separate phases in their development.

This means that the original links are now no longer an accurate representation of the interface as presented in the paper. To address this, the individual repositories were referenced under a single `meta repository' (Figure~\ref{fig:repo-relation}). Referencing is achieved using git's submodules functionality \cite{git_submodule_2025}. The benefit of such an approach is two fold.
To navigate the different states of the project in time, only a single URL needs to be shared. The individual submodules can be updated or swapped out entirely.
Version tagging is used to demarcate distinct versions of the interface, allowing for easier navigation.

The function of each component also shifted over time. The CAD component transitioned into files for electronic schematics and PCB manufacture, while the Model component has changed into a repository of documents describing measurements of the enclosure. Renaming either repository would break previous links to the work. The directory name of submodules can be changed in the meta repository, but this situation emphasised the importance of archiving the work.
Rather than referencing through URLs to a git repository, the project was archived using CERN's digital archiving platform Zenodo \cite{zenodo_2013}. Archiving exposed a few practical challenges. Zenodo’s GitHub integration does not yet handle meta-repositories, requiring separate DOIs for each submodule with cross-references \cite{zenodo_1036_2025} (Figure~\ref{fig:zenodo-github-integration}).
Likewise, the future of CAD data is tied to software sustainability: the discontinuation of Autodesk EAGLE \cite{autodesk_eagle_2024} effectively freezes its XML format, though conversion to KiCad remains available \cite{kicad_eagle_2024}. These lessons emphasise that openness depends not only on version control and archiving but also on the careful selection of formats and hosting infrastructure.

Documentation for the project still remains incomplete.
The firmware was documented as it was created using the Doxygen documentation system \cite{beningo_documenting_2017}, but this does not fill the role of a user manual \cite{stallman_gnu_1992,procida_diataxis_2025}.
Best practice emphasises the importance of documentation \cite{smith_software_2016}, though it is less prescriptive about form and depth.
\citet{writethedocs_sowftware_2025} offers comprehensive guidance. 
A pragmatic rule is that documentation should enable you to still ``understand your code in six months'' \cite{writethedocs_sowftware_2025}, a principle echoed
in reproducibility studies \cite{perkel_challenge_2020}.

The review of open research practice at NIME in Section~\ref{sec:current-state} highlights a growing trend of adopting open source projects within the proceedings at NIME, although the rate of open source usage does not match that of open source practice. 
The adoption-practice gap could be attributed to ambiguity on how to best disseminate project repositories.

The identification of the project source on a large scale is quite involved.

Moving forward one potential change that can be made to the NIME proceedings and paper submission process is the inclusion of a `project URL' field as a standard feature of the template and metadata (Figure~\ref{fig:project-details}). The benefits of such a change would be manifold.
To measure the impact of a NIME in a wider community something needs to be tracked.
A project's source repository and its URL provide a means by which it usage can be tracked, but identifying them in the first place is time consuming especially if it is to be rigorous.
Both \citet{calegario_documentation_2021, fiordelmondo_towards_2025} undertake very involved searches of the NIME archive to figure out if there is even a repository. It might be time to simplify the process of analysing open source practice at NIME. In the same manner that the NIME proceedings analyser can \cite{goode_toolkit_2022,nime2024_3} trivially extract authors and title data, the same should also be true for the location of the paper's source.

Table~\ref{tab:git_placement} illustrates that the manner in which source is shared is not standardised. Providing a standard field removes some burden from the author to interpret what would be the appropriate way to point to the work. In fact, a standard field would strengthen authorial intent as it removes ambiguity if a repository URL is the project source or simply related to the project in some manner.
This should hopefully reduce the growing number of instances where a project URL is invalid, either through the repository being behind authentication, set to `private,' empty or missing entirely, acting as it would a memory aid.

Asking authors for a project URL may also provide a reminder to share there work when a repository is extant, but the opportunity to reference it directly has not arisen or has been cut, as is all too easy during the paper writing and editing process. This can be enforces in the same manner as the \texttt{Description} field for figures of the current NIME paper template \cite{nime_template_2023}.

Whether this would make a difference, only implementation and time will tell.
The standard could be applied retroactively to the existing NIME archive.
The data collected as part of this paper and its state at time of submission are openly available \anon{\cite{hamilton_fair_2026, hamilton_fair_archive_2026}} to assist that process.

Finally, the burden is removed from the reader to identify where the source code for project is located. 
If the NIME community needs to put in a large amount of effort to find it, then the same will be true for the readership and a wider audience.
NIMEs provide a resource and archive for instructing students in creative engineering, electronics,  musicianship and HCI.
By making it harder for students and their instructors to engage \cite{nime2021_26}, remix \cite{dasgupta_remixing_2016} and foster creativity foster \cite{sharmin_creativity_2021, marin_computational_2024} NIME may be missing out on a new community of contributors.

Discussion of open source practice also raises broader issues of alignment with FAIR principles and digital project archiving. Given the distributed nature of git, there is more resilience to the problem of data loss. GitHub, as a standard, also has the infrastructure, but it should not be looked upon as a permanent storage solution, not for 20 or 30 years. The project source for a NIME needs to be citable \cite{smith_software_2016}, and cross-reference-able \cite{stall_journal_2023} if it is to be built upon. This is not just a salient topic for NIME, but all academic research. 

Digital archiving infrastructure exists in the form of \url{https://arxiv.org/} and \url{https://zenodo.org/} which are openly available. Zenodo is already utilised as the standard archive platform for NIME proceedings. 
For NIME 2025, 16 papers include video-only supplementary files, and one provides a citable digital archive with a DOI \cite{studley_swimtunes_2025}.
Those ``going beyond the PDF format'' \cite{calegario_documentation_2021} are still in the minority, and digital archiving remains an under-adopted standard practice. NIME has the potential to reach wider communities now, but it should also look towards the communities of the future.
